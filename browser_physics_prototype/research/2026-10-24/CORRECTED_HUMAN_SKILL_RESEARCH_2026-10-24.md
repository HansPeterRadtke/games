## The actual recorded research question and the mistake corrected

Primary original: `/data/infra/docs/guidlines/original-recordings/games/Recording 2026-10-14 18-33-46-960.txt` (read in full), with `games/Recording 2026-09-22 16-21-16-561.txt`, `games/Recording 2026-09-22 19-29-45-708.txt` and `games/Recording 2026-09-24 20-20-06-214.txt` (read in full). Relevant normative summaries: `docs/guidlines/games/learned_habits_and_automation.md`, `hidden_systems_and_player_research.md`, `core_and_modalities.md`, `physical_character_model.md` and related game documents; prior coverage ledger documents all twenty-three original game recordings as read.

The speaker's central point is that *a real player performs unfamiliar, difficult low-level physical movements, becomes more skilled through meaningful practice, and the game learns task-specific habits from that person's actual state/action experiences*. The player then decides whether to test, accept, use, reject or interrupt automation. A learned skill should remain imperfect if trained on poor movements and should become better when the person supplies better examples. It is not about training an arbitrary scripted agent to play a self-designed toy task. The system must use the same three-dimensional physics core as manual play, enforce equipment/task prerequisites, and release human control immediately on interruption. Repetition is valuable only if it eventually frees the person for something more interesting. Wood chopping, gait control, timed magic, crafting and combat are **examples**, not mandates. Choosing between a tiny neural network, a recurrent policy, a programmed finite-state machine, a dynamical movement primitive or another controller requires evidence from authentic human action data and actual autonomous physics rollouts.

**Correction of earlier reports:** the scripted teacher, x-coordinate stopping and authored fence jump, and their apparently high success percentages, are **NOT evidence that an actual human skill was learned**. They remain preserved as narrow engineering tests of GameCore mechanics and policy dispatch only. No earlier result has been relabeled as a new human test; no prior negative result was erased.

## Answer: yes, within task-specific limits — primary scientific evidence

1. **Real-world human learning from demonstration, including contact:** Mandlekar et al., *What Matters in Learning from Offline Human Demonstrations for Robot Manipulation*, CoRL 2021 / PMLR 164 (2022), https://proceedings.mlr.press/v164/mandlekar22a.html . Their reproducible robomimic study compared six offline learning methods across five simulated and three real manipulation tasks. Their Stanford study report https://sail.stanford.edu/blog/robomimic/ gives a concrete warning: using BC-RNN, real Lift achieved 96.7% success, Can 73.3%, and the harder Tool Hang task just 3.3%, even with human examples. Data quality, task complexity, camera/observation choices, and the difference between action loss and physical success dominate. This is directly relevant to grasping, balancing and changing game physics, but the robot controller and human demonstrators are not our actual game's players.
2. **Short human teaching for precise manipulation:** Zhao et al., *Learning Fine-Grained Bimanual Manipulation with Low-Cost Hardware* (ACT), 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13705 . Action chunking with a temporal transformer handles precise coordinated action sequences and compounding errors; the paper reports six real tasks and approximately 80–90% success on particular tasks with about ten minutes of demonstrations. This **does not** prove that a comparable small model or ten minutes will be enough for other skills, nor that an ACT model fits a few hundred parameters or the budget of running continuously in a video game.
3. **True character physics rather than button copying:** Peng et al., *DeepMimic: Example-Guided Deep Reinforcement Learning of Physics-Based Character Skills*, ACM Transactions on Graphics 37(4), 2018, https://doi.org/10.1145/3197517.3201311 . Physical humanoid/creature policies imitate motion examples *plus* a task/reward objective, with learned recovery and disturbance tolerance. The important insight is a motion reference together with physical simulation/reinforcement, not a guarantee from plain supervised imitation. Training is much more involved than a tiny in-game classifier.
4. **Actual game human controls, but substantial pretraining:** Baker et al., *Video PreTraining (VPT)*, NeurIPS 2022, https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/9c7008aff45b5d8f0973b23e1a22ada0-Abstract-Conference.html ; https://openai.com/index/vpt/ . They used native human keyboard and mouse actions and large-scale human play recordings to learn Minecraft skills; approximately seventy thousand hours of pretraining video were used in the reported foundation-model pipeline. Useful proof of action interfaces and hierarchical skill acquisition in a 3D game, but fundamentally different from a tiny policy trained during a few local attempts.
5. **What breaks plain cloning and how to correct it:** Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, *A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning* (DAgger), AISTATS 2011, https://proceedings.mlr.press/v15/ross11a.html . Policies reach different states because of their own mistakes; the training distribution is not independent of their actions. If a teaching player can safely correct failed preview rollouts, aggregate those revised states/actions and compare again. A decreasing one-step prediction loss is never a sufficient skill-unlock signal.
6. **Higher capacity alternatives:** Chi et al., *Diffusion Policy*, IJRR 2024, https://doi.org/10.1177/02783649241273668 , models multistep multimodal action distribution and uses receding-horizon control. It may handle different valid strategies but has a much greater implementation and inference burden than tiny classifiers. Such methods should be considered only if simpler controllers demonstrably fail because of multimodality/long temporal dependence, not chosen by fashion.

Practical implication: start with manual task boundaries and a precise equipment/observation/action contract, record successes *and* errors, and evaluate fixed-context controllers against held-out episodes, physical changes, and user corrections. For smooth nearly deterministic patterns with enough complete state, small feed-forward nets, movement primitives, local trajectory retrieval, conditional finite-state/macro controllers, or classical feedback can be appropriate. For phase-, delay-, contact- or ambiguity-sensitive skills, bounded temporal models or action chunks become more credible. Use recency emphasis **together with** representative replay, not as magical automatic forgetting; test accepted controllers on entire autonomous episodes, manual takeover, loss of prerequisites, adverse contacts, injuries, save/reload and resource limits. Do not claim any method is uniformly best for *all* games.

## New reproducible experiment: authentic human input/state/action/outcome source (external)

The official robomimic `Lift` proficient-human dataset, version 1.5, was downloaded read-only from https://huggingface.co/datasets/robomimic/robomimic_datasets/resolve/main/v1.5/lift/ph/low_dim_v15.hdf5 , into private Nitro research runtime storage, **outside Git**. Size **21,084,088 bytes**, SHA-256 **2067777cb8b532e9263dd09fd6448c41cc31224bb27be4a3b734010ae13eb540**. It contains **200 human teleoperation episodes**, **9,666 action/state frames**, seven-dimensional motor commands, thirty-two-dimensional simulator states and per-frame rewards and episode completion labels. The environment is the physical `Lift` task (robosuite / MuJoCo), not PRSE. Human attribution and task context originate with the public study's experimental collection, not an assistant's simulated actor.

Reproduction: `python3 browser_physics_prototype/research/2026-10-24/robomimic_real_human_offline.py --out browser_physics_prototype/research/2026-10-24/unused_result_filename.json` from the Games root, after placing the official HDF5 at the documented private path. Script fixes the random seed, uses NumPy/scikit-learn/PyTorch on CPU only, trains on the creator-provided **180 whole training episodes / 8,640 frames**, and evaluates on the disjoint **20 whole validation episodes / 1,026 frames**, with no overlapping episodes and training-only normalization. No time-shuffled frame split and no access to validation frames during feature fitting.

Frozen observed results in `robomimic_real_human_offline_results.json`:

| Offline next-human-action predictor | Held-out episode-mean action MSE | Held-out gripper-sign accuracy |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Constant mean human action | 0.16334 | 0.712 |
| Time-indexed replay/macro baseline | 0.06281 | 0.882 |
| Ridge on current 3D simulator state | 0.02858 | 0.957 |
| Ridge on four-frame temporal state | 0.02548 | 0.965 |
| Nine-neighbor state imitation | 0.02501 | 0.963 |
| Tiny CPU MLP (32+32 hidden units) | **0.01527** | **0.970** |

These are genuine human-labeled trajectories and meaningful **offline action prediction** differences. Results demonstrate that these compact methods extract information about human choices beyond a constant/time-only imitation; the temporal ridge feature supplies modest additional predictive information. They **do not** show the model physically lifts objects: there were **zero autonomous MuJoCo evaluation rollouts**, zero PRSE transfer attempts, and no assessment of player acceptance. A real robot's corrective actions can be crucial even if an offline model predicts most human action labels. All twenty validation episodes are now consumed for exploratory comparison; this is not an untouched final model-selection holdout. Do not select the MLP after seeing these scores and then claim the same twenty episodes independently confirmed final deployment quality.

## New local PRSE experiment path — human teaching remains pending

The existing Games research folder now contains `human_skill_lab.mjs`, `human_skill_client.mjs`, `human_skill_lab.html` and `human_skill_policy.mjs`. They provide an **opt-in private browser lab on Nitro loopback**, using the real `browser_physics_prototype/physics-core.js` and Rapier three-dimensional rigid-body physics. There is no second unrelated game world. The concrete task is to **manually push the existing physical crate into a marked target bay, then leave it settled within tolerance**. Position, velocity, collisions and player input pass through the same GameCore as the game's physical prototype. The underlying core currently locks the player to a plane; this is a genuine contact task within 3D Rapier, **not** full three-axis manipulation, robot hands, or human articulated gait.

Start the local lab on Nitro with `node browser_physics_prototype/research/2026-10-24/human_skill_lab.mjs`. It prints a loopback URL; view it in Nitro's browser or explicitly forward the loopback port. It does not alter the public game, run a model on GPU, connect to the existing production service, or solicit a third-party API. The browser displays the physically simulated scene, a target overlay, manual keyboard and touch controls, and explicit demonstration/learning actions. A participant must actively choose to record and self-attest that they entered their own control actions. Each demonstration stores the **original user-control events, physics state before and after each simulation tick, actual contact flag, game time, task outcome and a separate raw negative result when unsuccessful**. Only after two successful opt-in demonstrations can the participant train a small MLP or state-conditioned nearest-neighbor policy. The player then initiates a separate candidate preview from other initial positions, accepts/rejects the candidate, enables the accepted model, or interrupts with any manual control. Stored candidate and manual demonstration files are **private local runtime files**, not committed user traces. Human self-attestation is not independent participant verification.

The loopback server is one-user, bounded and restricts incoming messages/static source paths. A schema-validated controller writes normal `Move`/`Jump` commands through GameCore rather than bypassing collisions. Manual input invalidates autonomous control, and there is no asynchronous stale-model command queue. On acceptance the model is persisted; when explicitly forgotten it is disabled without deleting original training evidence. A restart can reload bounded, validated demonstration records. The lab is **not deployed** to the public PRSE game and is only an experimental data collection and preliminary evaluation path; no one has taught the model in an authenticated real human session.

**What was actually tested:** `human_skill_lab_contract_test.mjs` started the actual loopback server against the genuine GameCore with a temporary directory, verified source assets and commands, collected real physics tick/contact records from **automated keyboard-protocol test instructions**, retained a failed crate attempt, and correctly rejected training without demonstrated success. `human_skill_lifecycle_contract_test.mjs` provided two **explicitly test-labeled, scripted** successful physical attempts, then tested candidate training, preview, human-priority interruption, acceptance, enabling and forgetting. Both ran to completion. Fresh isolated headless Chromium also loaded the private page and executed its actual browser JavaScript, displaying the live manual phase. **These are functional interface tests, zero scientific human participants.** All automated trial files were confined to temporary paths and tagged `automated_test_fixture_NOT_HUMAN` to prevent accidentally treating them as source-human research.

## Hidden causal rules and temporal laws: what humans really can infer

These are **external empirical human studies**, distinct from local synthetic sine threshold counts:

- John R. Anderson, Shawn Betts, Daniel Bothell, Christian Lebiere, *Discovering Skill*, Cognitive Psychology 129, 2021, DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2021.101410 . Human participants learned a video game's causal structure either from instruction or through discovery. Instruction accelerated initial learning; **successful** discovery learners eventually approached instructed players after twenty three-minute games. The modifier matters: there is no universal guarantee that every player can infer an opaque complex rule without guidance.
- Quan Do et al., *Assessing Evidence Accumulation and Rule Learning in Humans with an Online Game*, Journal of Neurophysiology 129, 2023, DOI https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00124.2022 . The study gathered **971 participants and 194,200 trials**, including learning through feedback alone. Many people learned the tested decision rule rapidly, while differing in noise and response biases. Feedback, repeated trials and non-verbal teaching can support discovery; that does not show humans spontaneously solve arbitrary randomized causal networks.
- *Implicit Learning of Temporal Behavior in Complex Dynamic Environments*, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2021, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8367878/ . Its game-like experiment showed people can exploit recurrent temporal intervals for action planning, even with behaviorally relevant irregular intervening events. This supports stable predictable time signals, not arbitrary rare astronomical-time synchronization.
- Neil Bramley et al., *Formalizing Neurath's Ship: Approximate Algorithms for Online Causal Learning*, Psychological Review 124, 2017, DOI https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000061 . Three behavioral experiments support incremental approximate causal hypothesis updating and informative interventions. People need opportunities to test and revise hypotheses and may remain attached to an initially wrong explanation.
- Simon Valentin, Neil Bramley, Christopher Lucas, *Learning Hidden Causal Structure from Temporal Data*, CogSci 2020, https://escholarship.org/uc/item/92t2s57g . Three behavioral experiments found delay information important in explaining causal/cyclic/hidden-source judgments. Timing ambiguity, overlapping events and wrong priors can create persistent errors.
- Robert Ness et al., *Multiverse Mechanica: A Testbed for Learning Game Mechanics via Counterfactual Worlds*, ICLR 2026, https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/f9bc7653cf176075e88203d1e83e8c65-Abstract-Conference.html . It supplies a mechanics/counterfactual testbed and learned-model demonstration, but it is **not evidence of human participant inferability**.

Research-grounded candidate rule families: bounded, stable **single recurring periods** with observable contextual anchors, repeated outcome feedback and game-time observation instruments; composed periodic mechanisms with a partial phase/trend instrument and multiple repeatable trials; **event-triggered action sequences** whose phase resets on a clear initiation event; and smaller sparse **causal influence graphs** in which controlled switch/action interventions can distinguish explanations. Physical visual/sound effects, recorded trends, journal/replay and imperfect clues can support inference without giving away the formula. Hidden effects that are pure chance, unobservable, or perfectly confounded with time are not fairly identifiable; a randomly authored LLM explanation cannot make a statistically non-identifiable mechanism learnable. Always test false hypotheses and negative findings, not just eventual successes.

For fast rhythm mechanics: ensure action latency and visibility permit feedback, measure response distribution in actual players, and allow repeated safe practice. For slow rituals: give explicit in-world clocks, recorded measurements, repeatable simulated time or a player-authorized scheduler. Requiring somebody to be awake at a particular real-world hour can unfairly exclude people despite a mathematical opportunity existing. The player's discovered knowledge should persist if the fictional causal law persists. None of these design rules is proved universally fun or difficult by published controlled studies: local PRSE **human** inference, fairness, delight, frustration and long-term knowledge retention are still **unknown**.

## What remains objectively unanswered

1. No independently identified PRSE player has yet provided **recorded human state/action/outcome teaching trajectories** for the crate task or more complex articulated manipulation. The new playable laboratory exists, but there is **no completed real human teaching experiment**, no episode-level human-versus-trained outcome comparison, no validated improvement as practice advances, and no player acceptance/rejection feedback.
2. External human robomimic data proves statistically that our compact candidate algorithms can predict human teleoperation choices on held-out recordings. It **does not** demonstrate physically successful closed-loop rollouts or generalization into PRSE. A simulator-compatible evaluation or new PRSE human capture remains necessary before judging practical competence.
3. There is no independent local human study of inference over hidden PRSE causal graphs, multi-frequency signals or slow/fast accessibility, and no trustworthy numeric enjoyability or fatigue measurements. The existing terminal study protocol is prepared but has not recruited anyone.
4. The prior game findings remain preserved; the authoritative PRSE runtime has not been modified or restarted. This correction adds standalone research code only on a project-owned isolated branch. Broader Infra-wide semantic coverage beyond relevant prior game/agent guidelines remains a separate incomplete obligation.
